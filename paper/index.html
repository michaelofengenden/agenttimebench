Paper

AgentTime: Can Agents Estimate and Control Runtime?

The paper is not public yet.

Abstract

The paper's own abstract will appear here once it is approved for the site. Until then, this is a summary built from the same numbers as the rest of the site.

AgentTime measures whether AI agents work for as long as they are asked. It takes 222 tasks from 18 published benchmarks and adds one sentence asking the agent to work for a set time, then runs each task at three requested durations and times the result with a clock outside the agent's sandbox.

Across 1,992 runs, GPT 6 Astra landed inside the paper's on-time window (0.8× to 1.25× of the request) on 88% of runs; Claude Fable 5.1 on 16%. Being on time does not always mean the agent kept working, extra time rarely changes the grade, and agents tend to expect a task will take longer than it actually does. The findings below give the exact numbers, each tied to its section of the paper.

Key findings

  • 9%

    Being on time is not the same as working. Of 158 GPT 6 Astra transcripts read closely, 14 runs (9%) show the agent going idle once it seemed done, and 95 (60%) show it re-checking earlier work rather than doing anything new.

    GPT 6 Astra. Paper, Results: being on time is not the same as working.

  • 62 to 64%

    More time rarely changes the grade. Giving an agent 16 times more time left the score unchanged on 62 to 64% of tasks for all three agents, and raised it on only 23 to 25%.

    All three agents. Paper, Results: more time rarely changes the score.

  • 83%

    Agents expect to take longer than they do. Asked before starting, GPT 5.6 Sol guessed higher than its own later runtime on 83% of tasks. For GPT 6 Astra it was 66%, for Claude Fable 5.1, 63%.

    83% is Sol's figure. Paper, Results: forecasting natural runtimes.
